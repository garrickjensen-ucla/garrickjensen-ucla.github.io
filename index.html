Current research

MS thesis, in progress
VECTR Laboratory, UCLA
Advisor: Prof. Brett T. Lopez

Embodied LiDAR Signaling for RF-Silent Multi-Robot Persistent Search and Revisit

In mass-casualty incidents and search-and-rescue operations, initial assessment and triage are time constrained and often take place in dangerous, occluded environments. Autonomous robots are well suited to survey collapsed structures, disaster zones, and contested areas ahead of responders, but only if they can deploy without infrastructure, coordinate efficiently, and keep operating when communications are degraded or denied.

This thesis investigates whether a team of LiDAR-equipped aerial robots can cooperatively perform a persistent triage task, searching an unknown, obstructed, GPS- and radio-denied area for many casualties and repeatedly revisiting them for continuous health monitoring, coordinating only through a physical, LiDAR-based signaling channel and world-model-based reasoning.

This agent coordination problem was inspired by my work during the DARPA Triage Challenge, where setup was only allocated 20 minutes, required numerous cables and computers, and was prone to failure and gaps in communication. An autonomous drone could identify patient locations but lacked the ability to communicate them, interfering with our drones’ ability to coordinate with teaming robots. The communication infrastructure was time consuming, profoundly affected our UAV and UGV coordination and performance, and at times negated our autonomy during outages.

This research focuses on a physically grounded signaling-and-inference system for multi-agent coordination. Each robot carries retro-reflective markers that when switched periodically can encode a small discrete vocabulary as a temporal blink pattern visible to its teammates via LiDAR. I am investigating how a multi-agent deep reinforcement learning framework built on a DreamerV3-style Recurrent State-Space Model learns jointly to move, to signal, and to infer each observable teammate's intent from their observed signals and positions. I chose to use a model-based algorithm because encounters between teaming robots are sparse and therefore demand sample efficiency, and because its probabilistic latent can encode both its own localization and its teammates’ intents.

A team of N robots with M patients/targets, where M is much greater than N, operate in a bounded, obstructed arena in grouped but initially unknown locations. Dividing revisit responsibility is encouraged when patients greatly outnumber agents.

Agents do not have access to GPS or other sensors that correct their true location state, and never share maps. Each agent has a privately built map that is subject to drift error. An agent discovering a patient is unshared information unless the agent is within view of another agent that can detect its signaling.

As an algorithm and deep learning research engineer, I have worked with many different sensor modalities for various programs including underwater mine detection via water-penetrating LiDAR, high-dimensional searching for weak signals under time constraint via RL, and most of all autonomous triaging and vitals monitoring for mass casualty events.

For undersea mine detection I designed a novel DNN architecture that classifies spatial cubes with 2D transformers in place of 3D convolutions, working on undersea LiDAR from PILLS, Areté’s airborne Pushbroom Imaging LiDAR for Littoral Surveillance, reaching 0.97 AUC and beating 3D ResNeXt baselines at lower computational cost.

For autonomous robotic triaging, I worked on AIMS, Areté’s Automated Image-based Monitoring System, which locates and triages casualties from stand-off ranges on aerial and ground platforms. Specifically, I owned end-to-end training, validation and real-time deployment of joint human pose, body-part, and skin-segmentation models. Additionally I created robust algorithms for extracting heart and respiration rate information from FMCW radar and EO/IR imagery using real and Fourier-domain signal processing, for a multimodal non-contact vitals monitor.

In support of AIMS, I worked with medics and field-tested in simulated military exercises such as Project Capstone Convergence, Northern Strike, and competed in the DARPA Triage Challenge. I have had the opportunity to present results at the Military Health System Research Symposium for multiple years.

Lastly, I have worked on training reinforcement learning agents deployed on drones for time-constrained coverage optimization, searching for patients and then revisiting them. The reward makes the policy injury-aware: each patient's need to be revisited grows with time since the last visit, and grows faster the more severely they are injured.

Doosan Bobcat

Capstone Team Lead and Robotics Engineer

During my senior year capstone project at Harvey Mudd, I worked with Doosan Bobcat to create path planning and state estimation algorithms for an autonomous grass mower, meant to cover grass fields in a nice clean boustrophedon pattern. As team lead, I led a team of five students, set our agenda, managed our budget and group tasking, and liaised with Doosan, all alongside my technical work. I personally created a novel path planning algorithm for optimized boustrophedon curves using a modified traveling salesman problem, filed as WO2023205244A1. I also designed an Extended Kalman Filter utilizing radar and GPS data to perform localization, and implemented a particle filter for object detection and avoidance.

My path planning work was focused on creating an optimized schema for decomposing a field that may have large obstacles such as buildings, water, and trees or shrubbery. The area is first divided into cells whose boundaries fall at the obstacles, so that each cell is a simple region a mower can sweep without turning around an obstruction. We would rotate the field to find orientations that minimized the number of cells that needed to be created. The cells are then ordered as a tour over their centers, and only inside each cell does the actual back-and-forth mowing pattern get generated.

Selected projects

Fall 2021
Motion Genesis · MATLAB
STM32 · Max1000 FPGA

Multi-degree-of-freedom robotic arm

Rigid Body Dynamics × Microprocessor Systems and Design

In my senior year I combined the final projects for two different classes, one mechanical and one electrical, into a single design: a robotic arm that draws shapes. I started by modeling the dynamics of a three-segment arm with loop equations that would become the basis for solving the joint rotations. I wrote the loop equation for the marker tip across four reference frames, specified the target shape as a function of time, and solved the resulting system of nonlinear differential equations for the joint angles. Applying the angular momentum principle segment by segment gave the torque each motor would need.

Driving three steppers so the motion looks simultaneous is a scheduling problem, since each motor can only take discrete steps. The controller computes how many minimum steps each joint needs for the current time slice, finds the largest, and interleaves the three by issuing each motor a step every nth iteration.

The original three-arm configuration was too heavy for the motors that we could afford within the project budget ($100 total). The model had treated the motors as point masses and left out the horizontal load path, and the real motors were heavier than assumed, so motor B could not raise motor C. The last-minute fix was to remove the third link, rewrite the loop equation for two arm segments, and swap the marker for a laser pointer.
